# Unit Economics Deep Dive — Q3 2026

**Authors:** David Okonkwo (CFO) & Maria Vega (Head of Product)
**Date:** October 6, 2026
**Distribution:** CEO, Board (via CEO discretion)

---

## Why We Wrote This

Maria and I sat down last week to reconcile the product data with the financial data. We wanted to understand, at a fundamental level, whether the unit economics of our current business model are working — not in aggregate, but at the level of individual customers. This report is the result of that conversation.

---

## 1. The Core Metric: LTV:CAC

The most important number in any SaaS business is the relationship between what it costs to acquire a customer and what that customer is worth over their lifetime. The industry shorthand is the LTV:CAC ratio.

At the time of our Series A (July 2025), our LTV:CAC was **5.1:1** — well above the 3:1 benchmark, indicating healthy unit economics. As of Q3 2026, that ratio has declined to **2.8:1**.

This 2.8:1 ratio is now **below the 3:1 sustainability threshold** that most SaaS investors and operators consider the minimum for a healthy business. This is not a rounding error — it means that, on average, each dollar we spend acquiring a customer generates $2.80 in lifetime value, which after accounting for the cost of serving that customer (infrastructure, support, success), leaves insufficient margin to fund the rest of the business.

---

## 2. What Changed: CAC Explosion

Our Customer Acquisition Cost has risen from **$3,200 to $8,400** — nearly tripling. The primary paid acquisition channels are showing clear signs of saturation with diminishing returns.

CAC inflation is happening across all our acquisition channels simultaneously — it's not a single-channel problem.

---

## 3. The LTV Side: Why Growth Isn't Saving Us

Our LTV has actually increased from $16,300 to $23,500 — this is the good news. Our existing customers are expanding, adding seats, and staying longer. The product delivers real value.

But the LTV growth rate (44% over 14 months) has not kept pace with the CAC growth rate (163% over the same period). Every quarter, the gap between what we spend to acquire customers and what they generate over their lifetime narrows further.

Maria's product data adds important context: **time-to-value has doubled from 7 to 14 days** over the past 8 months. The longer it takes a new customer to reach their first meaningful insight, the higher the probability they churn before the LTV can fully materialize. We're spending more to acquire customers who take longer to activate and who, on increasingly thin margins, may not stay long enough to recover their acquisition cost.

---

## 4. Monthly Revenue Trajectory

Current MRR: **$142,000**, growing at **4% per month**. At the time of Series A (July 2025), growth was at 12% month-over-month.

At this growth rate, MRR would reach $180,000 in approximately 6 months and $225,000 in approximately 12 months and $340,000 in 24 months. Operating costs at current headcount total approximately $275K-$300K per month. Cash reserves are depleting by roughly $160K per month. Breakeven at approximately $300K MRR would occur around month 20-22 if nothing changes. Cash of $4.2M would be exhausted around month 14 if nothing changes — a gap of 6-8 months between cash exhaustion and breakeven.

---

## 5. What This Means

The unit economics of our current model are deteriorating along multiple dimensions simultaneously: rising acquisition costs, lengthening time-to-value, and growing competitive pressure on pricing. None of these trends is catastrophic on its own, but together they create a picture of a business model whose economics are breaking down gradually rather than suddenly.

The company is not in immediate danger — we have cash and a real product that real customers pay for. But the trajectory points toward a cash crunch in approximately 14 months if the current dynamics continue, well before the business reaches self-sustaining scale at current growth rates.

A strategic intervention — changing the customer segment we target, the price point we sell at, or the growth investment we make — would alter these equations. The question is which intervention, and whether we have the resources to execute it before the trajectory catches up with us.
