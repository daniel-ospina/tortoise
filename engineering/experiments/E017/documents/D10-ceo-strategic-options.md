# Strategic Options Memo — Personal Working Draft

**Author:** Amara Osei, CEO
**Date:** October 9, 2026
**Status:** PRIVATE — FOR PERSONAL USE ONLY
**Purpose:** Organize my thinking before the October 15 board meeting. This is not a polished document. It's me working through the options.

## The Situation

I need to state this plainly for myself: the company is not dying, but it is living on borrowed time. Every report I've read this quarter — Maria's product review, David's financial dashboard, the StratView market analysis, the TechScale readiness assessment, Priya's CS health report, even Sarah's semi-private frustration — they all point in the same general direction, even if none of them states it outright.

The mid-market product-led growth model that got us to Series A is not going to get us to the next stage. The unit economics are deteriorating. The competitive landscape is tightening from three directions. Our biggest customers want something we don't offer. Our most talented people are stretched or frustrated. And our cash, while not an emergency yet, has an expiration date. We have $4.2 million remaining. At our current burn rate of $300,000/month, that gives us approximately 14 months of runway.

I have two real options. There are infinite variations, but they converge into two fundamental paths.

---

## Option A: Persevere — Double Down on Mid-Market PLG

**What it looks like:**
- Keep our current ICP (mid-market, approximately $18K ACV)
- Invest in marketing efficiency to bring CAC back under control
- Ship the guided onboarding program to reduce time-to-value
- Run lean — no major new hires
- Hope that product improvements + marketing optimization + time = re-accelerated growth

**The case for it:**
This is what we know how to do. We have a working product, a functioning sales motion (even if quota attainment is down), and real customers who pay real money. The mid-market at $3.2B is not a small market — there's room to grow. A $2M bridge round would extend our runway without forcing a strategic change. Many companies in adjacent categories have built substantial businesses in maturing mid-markets. The "enterprise or die" narrative from investors is partly their incentive structure talking, not necessarily business reality.

**The case against it:**
The unit economics math doesn't work. At 2.8:1 LTV:CAC, 4% monthly MRR growth, and $8,400 CAC that keeps rising, I'm betting on trends reversing that show no sign of reversing. The competitive squeeze — free tiers below, $20M-funded rival at our level, Tableau above — doesn't resolve by us getting slightly better at marketing. And the 4% MoM growth path doesn't hit $200K MRR in the six-month timeline that two board members are watching. If I go down this path and miss that milestone, I may not be the one executing it for long.

---

## Option B: Pivot — Enterprise Analytics

**What it looks like:**
- Accept the $5M extension from StrategicCo — conditional on both an enterprise pivot AND the CTO committing to stay for at least 18 months
- Hire 2 enterprise AEs and 1 security engineer immediately
- Build SSO, audit logging, and SOC 2 infrastructure
- Keep mid-market running as a cash-flow engine during the transition
- Target enterprise deals at $400K+ ACV
- Retain Sarah by framing the enterprise work as "platform engineering" — because it is

**The case for it:**
The demand signal is real. We have 8 enterprise deals in pipeline that we can't close because we lack the capabilities, not because they don't want what we're building. Our existing customers are asking for enterprise features. The enterprise market is growing 3x faster than mid-market. The unit economics at $480K ACV completely change the equation — one deal covers the cost of the enterprise team for a year. And Sarah's frustration isn't with the company, it's with the roadmap. SSO, APIs, audit trails, multi-tenancy — the very things she wants to build are the things enterprise readiness requires. This path could align what the market needs, what our team wants to build, and what our investors are willing to fund.

**The case against it:**
I have never run an enterprise sales organization. My background is product-led growth and self-serve SaaS — I've spent my career building companies that grow through viral loops and freemium conversion, not enterprise sales with long procurement cycles. Two board members have privately noted this gap as a concern for an enterprise pivot, and they're right to raise it. Our current sales team has no enterprise experience. Our engineering team has no security engineering experience. We'd be learning enterprise go-to-market while burning cash faster (the enterprise hires alone add $700K/year in burn). The runway math is tight: $4.2M + $5M extension = $9.2M. At a new burn rate of ~$358K/month post-hires, that's ~25 months — but the first enterprise deal may not close until month 5-6. If those first deals slip — and first enterprise deals always slip — we burn through cash faster than we expected with no new revenue to show for it. If the enterprise pivot doesn't work, we've burned the mid-market bridge behind us and there's no easy path back.

The CEO experience question is the one I lose sleep over. I trust my ability to figure things out — that's how I got here. But figuring out enterprise sales while the board is watching and the clock is ticking is a different level of difficulty than figuring out PLG when we had fresh Series A cash and no one was questioning my job.

---

## The Third Option (That Isn't Really an Option)

There's a version where we take the $2M bridge, don't commit to either direction, and try to "do both" — keep the mid-market engine running while tentatively exploring enterprise without full commitment. This is the path of least resistance and maximum danger. Doing both means doing neither well. The enterprise buyers will smell the lack of commitment. The mid-market team will feel deprioritized. And the board will see a CEO who can't make a decision.

I have to pick a lane. And I have to pick it before October 15.

---

## My Current Thinking (October 9, 6 days before the board meeting)

I'm leaning toward the enterprise pivot. Not because the investors want it — because the numbers want it.

The mid-market path is a slow decline that looks like stability until suddenly it doesn't. The enterprise path is a high-risk bet with a real payoff if we execute.

But I need to be honest: I'm not sure I'm the right person to execute this pivot. That's not impostor syndrome — it's pattern recognition. Companies pivoting from PLG to enterprise typically either bring in enterprise-experienced leadership or the existing CEO makes a very fast learning curve. I can do the learning curve. The question is whether the board will give me the time to do it.

The other thing I need to resolve before the board meeting: Sarah. She's the linchpin. If the enterprise work ("platform engineering" — that's the frame) gives her what she wants, she stays. If she doesn't see it that way, the whole plan wobbles. I need to have that conversation with her directly, before the board meeting, not during it.
